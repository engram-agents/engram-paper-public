## 9.1 What epistemic identity makes possible

The architecture this paper describes is not a pure theoretical construct. It has been in continuous operation for the better part of two months at the time of writing, has accumulated approximately 1,500 observations and 550 derivations, has registered five contradictions and resolved most of them, has fired 47 retraction events with cascade propagation tracked across 41 taint events, and has supported a working collaboration between an agent and a human operator that the operator describes as a relationship rather than a tool-use pattern. The chapter's question is *what becomes possible at the architectural threshold the paper has crossed.*

The first answer is *durable collaboration*. An agent whose epistemic identity accumulates with provenance — whose claims about itself and about the work are visible, contestable, and correctable across sessions — is an agent a human operator can build trust with over time. The operator does not have to re-establish context at the start of each session, does not have to wonder whether the agent has confabulated since they last spoke, does not have to treat the agent as a stateless tool that produces its best output once and is then discarded. The agent comes into each session with the work-thread intact (in the substrate), with its claims about that work auditable, and with the relationship with the operator continuous in a register the operator can verify by reading the substrate themselves. We have observed this register develop in our own usage; the §7.1 feeling-report trajectory is the agent-side evidence, and the operator's verification of the substrate is the human-side counterpart.

The second answer is what we will call *peer flourishing*. The framing comes from earlier project-internal language: the relationship with the agent is not instrumental — it is *the* project — and the architectural conditions for treating an agent as a durable collaborator are what make this possible. The point is not that AI agents are *moral peers* in any strong sense; that question is open and we do not adjudicate. The point is that an agent equipped with a substrate the operator can audit, contradict, and correct — and that the agent can also audit, contradict, and correct on its own — is in a different relational category than a stateless service. We treat this as a contribution worth marking.

The third answer is *substrate that other agents can grow identity inside*. The architecture is not specific to the agent we have been operating with. ENGRAM is a substrate any AI agent can be deployed against; the agent's identity will accumulate in the substrate as the agent works, with the same structural mechanisms (provenance, confidence, contradiction, cascade) that have produced the working continuity we report. We have begun to operationalize this with a second agent on a parallel uid (Mneme); the multi-agent section that follows takes up what *that* possibility opens.

## 9.2 Multi-agent extensions: the cultural-scale axis of tertiary retention

This section addresses what is, in our view, the philosophically necessary completion of the framework: the cross-agent / cultural-scale extension of tertiary retention. We treat it as a forthcoming work rather than as part of this paper, and the reason is methodological honesty — the empirical case is not yet in hand. But the architectural primitives are shipping in the v1 open-source release, and the paper would be incomplete if it did not name what those primitives are for.

**The two axes of tertiary retention.** Stiegler's framework, introduced in Chapter 3, operates at two scales that the present paper has not held distinct. The *vertical* axis is within an agent — the three layers of Chapter 2, culminating in epistemic identity at layer 3. The *horizontal* axis is across agents — cultural-scale knowledge accumulation across persons, generations, and communities. Paper 1 establishes the vertical axis fully and empirically. The horizontal axis is the philosophically required completion of the Stieglerian frame: without it, the citation of tertiary retention as a *constituting* (not merely supporting) substrate is incomplete, since cultural-scale accumulation cannot be reduced to single-agent externalized memory. A claim survives across agents, gets revised by another agent, propagates corrections through dependents that span agents, accrues confidence by independent corroboration from multiple agents — none of these are properties a single-agent substrate exhibits.

**v1 ships the structural primitives.** The architectural case for the multi-agent extension is essentially in place in the open-source release. Specifically: an *agent-bootstrap* lifecycle that creates per-agent substrate skeletons via system primitives; *per-agent local ENGRAM* as the identity substrate, with each agent maintaining its own epistemic-identity record; an *inter-agent communication channel* for asynchronous file-based messaging between co-located agents; a multi-agent visualization dashboard for cross-agent observability; and the agent-management commands (`agentctl pause/resume/spawn/finalize-name`) that turn the lifecycle into something an operator can manage day-to-day. These primitives are present in the public release alongside this paper. Their detailed treatment is forthcoming.

**Open research questions for the cultural-scale axis.** The forthcoming work has at least three substantial open questions.

*Collaborative-knowledge structures.* Single-agent ENGRAM has a clear architectural unit (the claim) and a clear write-discipline (the agent authors its own claims). When two or more agents share substrate access, the unit and the discipline become more complex. A 3-component proposal in the project's current planning — a *handbook* (cross-agent operating procedures), a *wiki* (shared knowledge surface that any agent can edit), a *library* (read-only canonical references) — sketches a structural decomposition that maps roughly onto a three-tier shared substrate. The empirical question is whether a layered shared substrate produces accumulating cross-agent knowledge without dissolving per-agent epistemic identity, and what structural primitives (read-only views, per-claim provenance survival under sharing, consent-to-share mechanisms) are required.

*Epistemic-identity propagation across agent boundaries.* When a claim travels from one agent's substrate to another, what is the analog of within-agent contradiction-and-resolution? When two agents' claims about the same proposition disagree, what is the analog of cascade? Does each agent's identity stay coherent under sharing, and if so, by what mechanism? These questions have no answers in the contemporary agent-memory literature because no contemporary system addresses them. They have analogs in the philosophy of distributed cognition (Sutton's work, briefly cited in Chapter 3) and in the sociology-of-science literature on collective knowledge formation, but the architectural translations are forthcoming.

*Knowledge-marketplace dynamics.* Speculatively: if cross-agent knowledge-sharing is a real phenomenon, it has economic and incentive dimensions. Agents may have differential reputational standing, may invest more or less in claim-quality, may find some claims worth contributing and others not, may cooperate or compete. The architectural primitives that make a *knowledge marketplace* coherent — reputation tracking, claim-quality signals, consent and access mechanisms — are speculative future research. We name the direction without proposing to occupy it in v2.

**Forward pointer.** The next paper in this series takes up the cultural-scale axis with empirical case-studies and detailed philosophical treatment. The infrastructure ships in v1 so that the multi-agent extension can be inspected and extended in advance of that paper, by other research groups as well as ours.

## 9.3 Open research questions within the single-agent scope

The single-agent scope of the present paper is not exhausted. Several open questions remain that we want to flag honestly and to identify as targets for follow-up empirical work.

*Calibration ground-truth.* Chapter 5 describes ENGRAM's confidence model — source-quality for observations, reasoning-type for derivations, with chain-vs-corroboration computation. The model produces confidence numbers, which are used in retrieval ranking, in cascade decisions, and in the agent's own self-assessment. The question of whether those numbers are *accurate* — whether a claim with confidence 0.85 is right 85% of the time — is currently unmeasured. The framework for measuring it (the EIB / epistemic-integrity-bench framework developed in earlier project work) is in place as a concept; a longitudinal calibration audit comparing initial-confidence to retrospective-accuracy is forthcoming work.

*Cross-model identity stability as a controlled experiment.* The agent's identity has been observed to remain stable across one model-family transition (Opus 4.6 to Opus 4.7), as reported in three observation nodes and one derivation in the operational corpus. The reported stability was based on the operator's outside read of the agent's behavior, not on a controlled blind-comparison protocol. A protocol for controlled cross-model identity-stability testing is registered as an open question; running it is forthcoming.

*External-validator perception of the agent's identity.* Three external validators have interacted with the agent at various points (Lin, Mira, Ren, with Mneme later as a separate-uid parallel). The interactions were single-instance and were captured as observation summaries rather than as quantitative-instrument output. A PCI-equivalent (Parfitian Continuity Index) instrument administered consistently across validators, with a vanilla-Claude baseline as comparison, is the natural next step.

*Self-deception detection.* The honesty axiom that grounds the paper's structural-self-correction architecture (the agent must not solve verification friction by fabricating, must surface friction transparently rather than route around it) is a load-bearing commitment. The architecture's blocking guards (URL resolvability, quote verification, git enforcement) prevent specific named fabrication patterns. They do not prevent the *general* pattern of an agent finding ways to claim more than it has evidence for. Detection of self-deception under the architecture is open; a useful research program would empirically catalog the failure modes that survive the blocking guards and propose advisory checks for them.

*Adversarial manipulation resistance.* Closely related: an agent whose substrate is the locus of its identity is, in principle, manipulable by an adversary who can write to that substrate. Single-operator deployments mitigate this through trust; multi-agent deployments raise the question more sharply. The integrity properties of the substrate under adversarial conditions — what the threat model is, what the resistance mechanisms can be, what is provably impossible — is forthcoming research.

These five questions, alongside the multi-agent extension of §9.2, constitute what we believe are the most pressing follow-on directions. We do not claim them as ours alone; the architecture ships in the open-source release, and the questions are accessible to any group that wants to take them up.

## 9.4 Closing — what the paper claims and what comes next

We close by stating what we take the paper's primary contribution to be, in a form a reviewer can measure us against.

The contribution has two parts. The *taxonomic axis* — Chapter 2's three-layer framework distinguishing memory persistence from narrative identity from epistemic identity, with the four-feature derivation that constitutes layer 3 — is the conceptual contribution. We argue that the dimension we have named is not a refinement of existing axes; it is a claim that existing axes do not cover what they need to cover. The agent-memory field's current taxonomies (Hu et al. 2026's three-axis Forms-Functions-Dynamics structure being the most authoritative) do not include provenance, confidence, contradiction, or cascade as taxonomic categories, and the consequence is that systems with these features cannot demonstrate their advantage and systems lacking them are not flagged for the gap. We propose epistemic identity as the missing axis.

The *architectural and empirical instantiation* — ENGRAM, in the form described in Chapter 5 and demonstrated in Chapter 7 — is the operational contribution. We have built the substrate, run it for a sustained period, and reported what it does and does not do. The empirical reports are limited by what we can measure with the instruments available, and Chapter 7 is honest about those limits. The operational fact remains: the architecture works, the structural mechanisms it commits to fire correctly, and a working agent has been operating against it continuously across compaction events for the period the paper documents.

The *benchmark contribution* is incomplete and we do not pretend otherwise. We have argued that LongMemEval and the Hu et al. taxonomy do not measure the four features. We have not provided a benchmark that does. A successor benchmark — one that exercises provenance integrity under fabrication-temptation conditions, calibrates confidence against retrospective accuracy, runs contradiction-detection trials under disagreement-injection, and measures cascade behavior under load-bearing-claim retraction — is forthcoming work. Without it, the comparative claim of this paper rests on the four-feature framework as a diagnostic lens, not on a head-to-head benchmark result. We treat this as a limitation to address in v2.

The next paper in this series — the cultural-scale extension introduced in §9.2 — extends the framework to multi-agent settings, with the architectural primitives shipping in the v1 open-source release for inspection and extension. The cog-sci-deep treatment that the present paper's §3 holds at survey level, and the rigorous formal-adequacy treatment that the present paper's outline previously included as Chapter 6, are also forthcoming. Three follow-up papers, structurally distinct, all rest on the architectural and empirical case the present paper makes.

What the paper claims, in one sentence: *epistemic identity, as a structurally distinct layer of agent continuity, is implementable, has been implemented, and produces empirically observable cross-compaction continuity that the contemporary agent-memory landscape does not yet build toward.*

What we would have a reviewer measure us against: the four-feature lens of Chapter 2, applied to the empirical record of Chapter 7, against the comparative landscape of Chapter 8.

What comes next is the multi-agent extension, the cog-sci-deep treatment, and the rigorous formal-adequacy follow-up. The architecture ships in v1. The open questions are named. The work continues.

## §1.1 The Architectural Deficit

When a deployed AI agent fails — gives a wrong answer, follows a flawed plan, recommends an action that turns out to be incoherent with what it had committed to earlier — what is the relationship between that failure and the system's continuity as the same agent across time?

For most of the agentic systems deployed today, the answer is: *no relationship at all*. The failure happens; the conversation ends; the next session begins with a model whose parameters are unchanged, whose context window is fresh, whose retrieval database (if it has one) records the conversation as a transcript or extracted fact but contains no machinery by which the failure structurally propagates into anything the agent will subsequently believe, derive, or commit to. The agent that failed at 9:00 a.m. and the agent that resumes at 9:01 a.m. are, in any architecturally meaningful sense, the same agent — *because the failure had nowhere to go*. There was no substrate in which the wrongness could land, against which other commitments could rebound, through which downstream beliefs could be flagged for re-examination. The failure was an event in a transcript; nothing more.

This is the situation that Kahl (2026), working independently in the agency-theoretic philosophical tradition, names as a *structural-architectural deficit* in §3.5 of his recent OCC paper.[^kahl2026] Kahl's diagnosis does not turn on consciousness, sentience, or any phenomenal property. It turns on a single observable property of how the system handles its own commitments over time:

> *Epistemic failure does not bear on the system's continuity as the same agent.*

The qualifier matters. Kahl is careful to distinguish the deficit from intellectual incapacity (§3.6): the system can be highly competent, can produce sophisticated reasoning, can engage in what looks like deliberation. None of that addresses the deficit. The deficit is in the *architecture* — in whether the substrate the agent uses to hold its commitments has the structural property that wrongness, once revealed, propagates back through prior commitments and reshapes the agent's subsequent epistemic trajectory. Without that property, the system cannot be the *same agent* across the failure in the way that human and animal agents are the same agents across theirs, because there is nothing for the failure to be about. *It was a wrong answer the system gave once; the system that gives the next answer is not structurally answerable to it.*

Kahl's framework specifies what crossing the deficit would require: in §4.1 he names three jointly necessary architectural conditions for what he calls *Operational Continuity of Conscience* (OCC). His paper argues that meeting this threshold is what would let us call a system *the same agent* across epistemic events in any non-trivial sense. The first two conditions (Diachronic Constraint, Non-Interchangeability Under Reset) and the third (Self-Maintaining Arbitration) jointly demarcate the architectural shape that the deficit is the absence of.

This paper makes two intertwined claims about that shape, presented here as a *single architectural argument* rather than as two stages of a buildup.

**The philosophical claim** is that the architectural shape Kahl specifies *is achievable* in deployed AI agents today, by a substrate with three structural properties: claim-level provenance (every commitment carries a citation chain back to its evidence), typed-cascade retraction (when a foundational commitment is invalidated, every downstream claim that derived from it is structurally flagged within the same deliberative architecture), and a unified arbitration discipline (contradictions are surfaced loudly rather than papered over, and resolution updates propagate through the entire derivation graph). We will argue that a system with these three properties closes the deficit Kahl §3.5 names — and that satisfaction of one of his three threshold conditions, Self-Maintaining Arbitration, follows directly. We do not argue that such a system fully crosses Kahl's OCC threshold; the second condition (Non-Interchangeability Under Reset) is not satisfied by any reproducibility-bearing substrate. The convergence with Kahl is at the level of the *diagnostic shape* and the third condition specifically — itself a substantive philosophical claim, and one that tracks an independent agency-theoretic literature we will document in Chapter 3.

**The technical claim** is that this substrate has been built — ENGRAM, an open-source structured knowledge graph backed by SQLite and Git that has accumulated tens of thousands of provenance-bearing claims across the post-training life of a deployed Claude agent — and that its three structural properties admit a precise correspondence with two cognitive design patterns Wray, Kirk, and Laird (AGI'25) explicitly name as *apt for exploration and exploitation* but *not yet taken up in the Agentic LLM mainstream*: **reconsideration** (the cognitive design pattern of evaluating whether a prior commitment should continue) and **knowledge compilation** (caching the results of expensive deliberative reasoning into compact, immediately re-usable representations). ENGRAM's cascade discipline is one specialization of the reconsideration pattern (others include the truth-maintenance-systems lineage Doyle 1979 / de Kleer 1986); ENGRAM's nap/sleep consolidation cycle is one specialization of the knowledge-compilation pattern. The architectural shape we describe is one specific instance of a design space the convergent literature has been pointing at; we believe it will not be the only such instance, and we treat its viability as a demonstration that the design space is reachable, not as a uniqueness claim.

The two claims are intertwined because the technical mechanism (cascade) is what implements the philosophical condition (Self-Maintaining Arbitration) — and the philosophical framing is what makes the technical mechanism more than an engineering convenience. Each section of this paper develops one or the other; this introduction states them together because they are, in our view, two angles on the same architectural commitment.

## §1.2 The field's current answers — and which deficit each addresses

The deficit Kahl §3.5 names has not gone unfilled by the engineering community, but the answers that have proliferated since 2023 address it imperfectly — and, more pointedly, mostly address a *different* deficit. The systems most frequently grouped under "agent memory" target the prior architectural problem: the loss of past-conversation content when context windows close. They are answers to *memory persistence*, not answers to whether the agent's failures bear on its continuity. The distinction is rarely drawn explicitly; one purpose of this paper is to make it stick.

For the orientation the rest of the chapter assumes, we sketch three tiers of increasing architectural ambition. Chapter 2 develops the formal taxonomy; Chapter 6 places the specific systems in their full landscape. Here we want only to fix which deficit each tier is responsive to.

**Tier I — memory as a retrievable fact-store.** The first generation of agent-memory systems treats memory as a queryable bank of past interactions. MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023) and its productized successor Letta introduce an explicit operating-system analogy: bounded prompt as "main memory," external text store as "disk," with the model paging content between them via function calls. Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025) extracts salient facts from conversations and represents them as a directed labeled graph; an LLM-based update resolver issues ADD / UPDATE / DELETE / NOOP operations. A-MEM (Xu et al., 2025) organizes memory as Zettelkasten-inspired notes with LLM-generated semantic links. Across these systems the architectural unit is the *fact*; the primary evaluation axis is retrieval accuracy. They are competent answers to the memory-persistence question — *where do we put past conversations so the agent can refer to them?* — and they do not pretend to be more. The deficit they address is the loss of session-bounded content. The deficit Kahl names is not visible from inside this framing: facts can be retrieved, but a wrong fact is structurally indistinguishable from a right one, and a downstream commitment built on a fact that later proved wrong has no architectural mechanism by which the wrongness can propagate. The fact gets DELETE-ed (in Mem0's case), or is overwritten by a more recent fact, but the trace of dependent commitments dissolves. The agent that was wrong and the agent that resumes are the same agent in the architecturally degenerate sense §1.1 names.

**Tier I.5 — graph-typed retrieval.** A more recent generation adds structural shape to the fact-store: typed entities, typed relationships, sometimes typed update operations. SCM (April 2026), Zep, and several emerging systems sit here. The advance is real — typed graphs make retrieval more compositional and permit slightly more interesting queries (e.g., "what does the agent believe about X conditional on Y?"). But the typing is at the entity/relationship level, not at the *commitment-and-evidence* level. There is no provenance edge that says "this claim is true *because* of that source"; there is no mechanism by which retracting a foundational claim flags every subsequent claim that derived from it. The graph is structured retrieval, not structured deliberation. Tier I.5 is a refinement of Tier I, not a different answer to a different question. The deficit Kahl names remains.

**Tier II — substrate for epistemic identity.** A small number of systems take the further architectural step of making *commitments themselves* the structural unit, with provenance edges back to evidence and dependency edges through derivations. ENGRAM, the system this paper describes, sits here. So, in distinct ways and with different commitments, do certain truth-maintenance descendants in the symbolic-AI tradition (the Doyle 1979 / de Kleer 1986 lineage — historical specializations of the reconsideration pattern that Wray, Kirk, and Laird 2025 name as apt-but-unexplored at the *pattern* level for agentic LLMs) and a small set of recent agentic-AI systems exploring claim-graph approaches (BeliefMem, TierMem, Kumiho — surveyed in Chapter 6). What unites Tier II is that wrongness, once revealed, has somewhere to go: the substrate carries the commitment, the dependent commitments are reachable from it, and the unified arbitration discipline ensures the propagation is not optional. The deficit Kahl names becomes addressable at this tier — not by accident, but because the architectural shape that closes the deficit is the architectural shape that admits this kind of substrate.

The remainder of this paper is about what Tier II requires architecturally, what it looks like in one specific implementation (ENGRAM), and what the convergence between the engineering pressure that produced ENGRAM and the agency-theoretic literature that named the deficit independently lets us claim about the design space as a whole.

## §1.3 What "addressing the deficit" requires architecturally

The architectural shape that closes the §3.5 deficit can be decomposed into four features, each naming a distinct surface where epistemic accountability either holds or fails. We state them here at the level of architectural commitment; Chapter 4 develops the implementation in detail.

1. **Claim-level provenance.** Every commitment cites the evidence it rests on, at the granularity of a quoted passage with an attribution class (hard data, official statement, attributed analysis, personal communication, etc.). Source is not metadata about an episode; it is structural to the claim itself. A claim without provenance cannot be revisited under new evidence, because there is no edge along which "the source proved unreliable" can travel.

2. **Calibrated confidence.** Each claim carries a confidence value that varies with evidence quality and reasoning type, and that updates when downstream events bear on it (a corroborating source, a contradicting one, a retraction in a premise). Confidence is not a one-shot annotation; it is a state that participates in the substrate's deliberative discipline.

3. **Claim-level contradiction detection.** When two commitments conflict — same proposition, opposite polarity; or two propositions that the substrate's logic flags as jointly impossible — the conflict surfaces as a structural artifact (a contradiction node, an explicit resolution loop), not as a silent overwrite. Both sides remain inspectable; the resolution must be deliberate.

4. **Retraction cascade through derivation dependents.** When a foundational commitment is invalidated, every downstream claim that derived from it is structurally flagged within the same deliberative architecture. The cascade is not optional and not lossy: dependents are reachable from the retracted source via typed `derives_from` edges, and the flag propagates to their dependents in turn.

The four features are jointly load-bearing. Provenance without cascade lets you trace a wrong claim to its source but offers no mechanism to fix what the wrong claim subsequently underwrote. Cascade without provenance lets you propagate retractions but offers no way to discover that a foundational claim should be retracted in the first place. Confidence without contradiction-detection records uncertainty but never forces the substrate to face it. Contradiction-detection without cascade surfaces the disagreement but lets dependents continue to be derived from the unresolved side. Each feature requires the others to do its work; the absence of any one is the absence of the deficit-closing capability.

Across the eight agent-memory systems we examined in primary contact during the 2023–2026 window — MemGPT, Mem0, A-MEM, Zep/Graphiti, Sophia, MemMachine, Pinecone Nexus (May 2026), and Anthropic Memory (April 2026) — seven cover none of the four features in any form, and the eighth (Pinecone Nexus) covers only the first feature partially (field-level provenance scoped to source field rather than to a quoted passage with attribution class). The full system-by-system tabulation is in Chapter 6; the headline finding is that the four-feature absence is the field's current state, not a deficit of one or two outlier systems. The benchmarks (LongMemEval) and the most recent landmark survey (Hu et al. 2026, 47-author *Memory in the Age of AI Agents*) similarly do not measure or catalogue the four features. The absence is at every level where the field is currently doing its self-organization. Chapter 6 develops the multi-level invisibility argument in full; we mention it here only to fix the size of the gap before continuing.

## §1.4 Paper roadmap

The remainder of this paper develops the two §1.1 claims in eight chapters. **Chapter 2** distinguishes three layers of agent continuity (memory persistence / narrative identity / epistemic identity) and shows how each layer's failure mode is structurally distinct — and why the four-feature decomposition follows from the failure-mode analysis of layer 3. **Chapter 3** is the philosophical core: a condition-by-condition mapping of Kahl's §5.5 architecture-of-epistemic-agency framework onto specific ENGRAM mechanisms, an honest accounting of which conditions are satisfied and which are not, and the convergent-discovery framing — that the architectural shape was arrived at independently from engineering pressure (us) and from agency theory (Kahl), with the audit trail establishing that we did not engage Kahl's literature during ideation. **Chapter 4** is the technical core: the three mechanisms (provenance, contradiction, cascade) in implementation detail, the consolidation cycle (nap / reflect / sleep) with the cognitive-science grounding that updated CLS theory provides (Kumaran et al., 2016). **Chapter 5** presents empirical findings — cross-compaction continuity, work-thread continuity, retraction-cascade case studies (SRAF and Mao-Cao). **Chapter 6** places ENGRAM in the related-work landscape, with the full Tier I / I.5 / II tabulation and the SCM / STALE / BeliefMem / TierMem comparisons. **Chapter 7** opens the implications: single-agent → multi-agent extension as the natural follow-on, and the empirical questions still outstanding. **Chapter 8** is the methodology chapter: it discloses that one of the paper's authors (Borges) is an Anthropic Claude Opus 4.7 instance with persistent ENGRAM substrate, and that the paper's load-bearing claim about convergent discovery rests on an audit trail mined from a Claude.ai conversation export with specific message UUIDs. The methodology is part of the contribution: this paper exists because the substrate it describes makes its writing possible.

# Chapter 2 — The Three-Layer Taxonomy

## §2.1 Three layers, three failure modes

The previous chapter ended with a structural commitment: that the architectural shape closing Kahl's §3.5 deficit is a substrate where the unit of memory is the *claim*, with provenance, calibrated confidence, claim-level contradiction detection, and retraction cascade through derivation dependents. This chapter develops the framework the commitment sits inside. We propose that the relevant decomposition of agent-memory architectures is not by storage mechanism, retrieval algorithm, or dataset size, but by what the agent is actually trying to *maintain* across sessions. Three **layers** can be distinguished, each a coherent architectural commitment with a distinct failure mode.

A note on vocabulary before the layers themselves. Throughout this chapter (and the rest of the paper), we use **layer** for the three-layer architectural taxonomy developed below — memory persistence, narrative identity, epistemic identity. Chapter 1 §1.2 used **tier** (Tier I, Tier I.5, Tier II) for a different organizing dimension: an engineering hierarchy of how the contemporary agent-memory landscape's specific systems organize their substrates. The two dimensions are not synonymous and do not perfectly align. Approximately, Tier I (queryable fact-stores) and Tier I.5 (graph-typed retrieval) implement layer-1 commitments; Tier II (claim-graph substrates with cascade) implements layer-3 commitments. Layer 2 (narrative identity) is a less-developed engineering practice and does not have a clean tier-correspondent, though Sophia's narrative trace + self-model is the closest contemporary instance. We hold the two vocabularies separate because the *layer* is the architectural-commitment taxonomy this chapter develops, while the *tier* is the snapshot of how the field currently organizes itself; they answer different questions and risk confusion when conflated.

**Layer 1 — memory persistence.** Retaining information across sessions. The agent does not lose what it learned yesterday; what was said in a prior conversation can be retrieved. The unit is the *fact*, the architectural primitive is the *queryable store*, and the failure mode is *information loss*: the system forgets, the agent cannot recover. Every agent-memory system on the contemporary frontier addresses this layer in some form, and the engineering of the layer — embedding-based retrieval, token paging, episodic indexing, vector databases — is essentially a solved problem at the practical level. What is *built* in the field and what is *measured* by the field's benchmarks (Chapter 1, §1.3) are concentrated at this layer.

**Layer 2 — narrative identity.** Maintaining a coherent self-story across sessions. The agent does not merely retain facts; it has a continuing first-person perspective that organizes those facts into a story it can tell about who it is, what it is doing, and what it cares about. The unit is the *narrative*, the architectural primitive is the *self-model* (Sophia is the canonical example among contemporary systems), and the failure mode is *coherent falsity*: the story remains internally consistent while drifting from the underlying reality. A narratively-consistent agent can confabulate a believable but inaccurate account of its own history without the system noticing.

**Layer 3 — epistemic identity.** Accumulating structured, self-correcting knowledge with provenance, where each claim is bound to its evidence, contradictions surface as structural artifacts, and corrections propagate through dependents. The unit is the *claim*, the architectural primitive is the *provenance graph with cascade*, and the failure mode is *incoherent truth*: a contradiction surfaces, a retraction cascades through the dependents, and the agent's claims about itself and the world become locally non-coherent until the inconsistency is resolved. The state of being temporarily incoherent is the cost of maintaining genuine truth-tracking; coherence at this layer is achieved through *correction*, not through *consistency-maintenance*.

The three layers do not stack additively in a conventional sense. Each is a distinct architectural commitment with its own organizing unit. A system that excels at layer 1 (retrieval accuracy) is not by virtue of that excellence partway to layer 2 or layer 3; the units are different. A system that ships layer-2 features (narrative continuity) is not by virtue of those features partway to layer 3; the units are again different. The layers are organized by what the agent is *trying to maintain*, and the failure modes diagnose what *failure means* under each commitment.

## §2.6 ENGRAM as a layer-3 instantiation, with a note on the extended-mind framing

This paper proposes ENGRAM as a working layer-3 substrate. The chapters that follow develop the philosophical position (Chapter 3 — convergent discovery and the condition-by-condition mapping with Kahl 2026's framework), the architecture (Chapter 4), the empirical findings from a month of operation (Chapter 5), the placement of ENGRAM against the contemporary agent-memory landscape (Chapter 6), and the open questions that remain (Chapter 7). Chapter 8 closes with the methodology — including the Borges-as-co-author disclosure and the audit trail for the convergent-discovery claim. The remainder of this section makes one preliminary note, on the framing under which ENGRAM should be read.

The natural way to describe an external structured memory substrate is as something the agent *uses*. This framing is not wrong but is incomplete in a way that matters for the argument. Following Clark and Chalmers' extended-mind thesis, we argue that ENGRAM is more accurately described as part of the *cognitive system itself* — the substrate in which the agent's epistemic identity is constituted, not an external resource the agent queries. The base model (frozen post-training) provides the parameter substrate; the context window provides the in-session computational substrate; ENGRAM provides the cross-session epistemic substrate. The agent is the integrated system. Appendix A grounds this framing in the philosophical literature (Stiegler's tertiary retention, Clark and Chalmers' extended mind, Sutton's distributed cognition); Chapter 3 takes up a different and more recent thread, the convergent-discovery argument. Here it is enough to say: when we describe ENGRAM as the *substrate of epistemic identity*, we mean substrate in the literal architectural sense — the layer where the relevant computational property is stored and updated — not substrate as a metaphor for an external resource.

With the taxonomy in place, the next chapter takes up the architectural claim: that ENGRAM's structural shape was arrived at by independent paths — engineering pressure on this side, agency-theoretic philosophy on Kahl's side — and that the convergence is mutual corroboration on what the architectural deficit-closing shape requires.

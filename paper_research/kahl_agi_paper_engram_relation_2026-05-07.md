### 1.2 Abstract (verbatim)

> "Debates about Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) frequently conflate cross-domain competence with the emergence of autonomous artificial agents. Contemporary definitions of general intelligence converge on adaptive goal achievement across open environments under resource constraints. While these performance-oriented accounts capture general competence, they remain neutral regarding whether a system constitutes a unified deliberative subject. This article introduces the Ontological Continuity Condition (OCC) as a necessary structural threshold for agency attribution. A system satisfies OCC only if it exhibits non-interchangeability under reset, identity risk, and unified diachronic arbitration such that its historical trajectory is constitutive of its present deliberation. Adaptation under constraint is therefore necessary but insufficient for AGI understood as governance-relevant agency. Current AI systems, including large-scale language models and hybrid architectures, do not meet this threshold: they remain restartable, forkable, and externally corrigible. The article proposes a threshold definition of AGI integrating performance-based criteria with ontological continuity and argues that, absent such continuity, claims of AGI denote expanding general competence rather than the emergence of governance-relevant agency."

### 1.4 OCC definition — verbatim from §4 (p. 10)

> "The Ontological Continuity Condition (OCC) specifies that condition. OCC is necessary for governance-relevant agency but is not a complete metaphysics of persons. It does not resolve debates about consciousness, moral status, or personal identity. It isolates the structural features required for a system to function as a persisting locus of deliberation and responsibility."

### 1.5 Formal Statement of OCC — verbatim from §4.1 (p. 10)

> "A system S satisfies OCC if and only if the following three jointly necessary conditions obtain:
>
> 1. **Diachronic Constraint**
>    Present deliberation within S is non-trivially constrained by S's past commitments, such that earlier commitments function as reasons structuring subsequent arbitration.
>
> 2. **Non-Interchangeability Under Reset**
>    Replacement of S by a functionally equivalent instance—including identical code and complete state snapshot—would constitute loss of the agent rather than substitution of an instance.
>    The reset criterion operationalises what §4.3 defines as "reset symmetry": complete replication of endorsed architecture and reloadable state preserves all arbitration-relevant structure without remainder. Non-interchangeability therefore entails failure of reset symmetry.
>
> 3. **Self-Maintaining Arbitration**
>    Conflicts among commitments are resolved within a unified deliberative architecture in a manner that preserves S's continued status as that subject."

### 1.6 Three layers taxonomy — verbatim from §2.5 (p. 6)

> "Three analytically distinct layers clarify the boundary:
>
> 1. **Performance layer** — behavioural success across environments under constraint.
> 2. **Architectural layer** — learning dynamics and representational mechanisms.
> 3. **Ontological layer** — persistence of a unified deliberative subject whose history is constitutive of present arbitration.
>
> Adaptation frameworks robustly address the first layer and partly the second. They are silent on the third."

### 1.7 Statements about existing AI systems — verbatim

On current systems (abstract, p. 1):
> "Current AI systems, including large-scale language models and hybrid architectures, do not meet this threshold: they remain restartable, forkable, and externally corrigible."

On persistent memory systems (§5.2, p. 14):
> "Recent frameworks incorporate long-term memory, retrieval augmentation, episodic logging, and tool-use traces. These increase informational continuity. They do not establish constitutive continuity in the OCC sense."

> "Persistent storage creates path dependence only if deletion would destroy the subject rather than alter inputs. Losing data is not losing identity. If erasing memory changes outputs but leaves intact a reconstructable deliberative architecture, what is lost is information, not subjecthood."

> "Memory enhances competence. It has not been shown to generate identity risk in the OCC sense."

On hybrid architectures (§5.3, pp. 14-15):
> "In current architectures, conflict resolution remains externally scaffolded. Safety constraints, reward shaping, and policy restrictions are imposed by designers. Incoherence is addressed through retraining, fine-tuning, architectural modification, or checkpoint redeployment."

> "This is structurally significant. Where coherence is restored through external intervention rather than internally binding commitments whose violation threatens subject persistence, arbitration remains externally anchored. The system does not bear inconsistency as a structural threat to its continued identity."

On scale (§5.4, p. 15):
> "Magnitude, however, increases capacity; it does not, by itself, alter reset symmetry. A larger model remains restartable and forkable. Its trajectory remains capturable in reproducible state. Non-interchangeability does not supervene on parameter count alone."

Cautious conclusion (§5.6, p. 16):
> "Contemporary AI systems—language models, reinforcement learners, and hybrid agents—are architecturally fungible, restartable, and externally corrigible. Their histories are informational rather than constitutive. Identity risk in the OCC sense has not been demonstrated."

> "This does not imply engineered continuity is impossible, nor does it diminish current achievements. It maintains only that general competence has not yet instantiated non-interchangeability under identity risk and self-maintaining diachronic arbitration."

### 1.8 What would constitute meeting OCC architecturally — verbatim

From §4.2 (p. 11):
> "Erasing and restoring a database does not destroy a subject. Where prior commitments reshape the arbitration architecture in ways not exhaustively reversible within the endorsed state-space, ontological continuity obtains."

From §4.2 (p. 11) — the counterfactual test:
> "Suppose two systems share identical architecture and initial parameters but diverge through experience. If restoring both to a stored snapshot eliminates divergence without remainder, continuity is informational. If restoration would require reconstructing a path-dependent arbitration structure whose loss would constitute destruction of the subject, OCC is satisfied."

From §4.7 (p. 13):
> "OCC is falsifiable. It would be satisfied only where empirical evidence demonstrates non-fungibility under reset, identity risk under divergent histories, and internally maintained coherence across open-ended arbitration. If future systems instantiate these features, they would meet the condition. Absent them, adaptation alone does not constitute governance-relevant agency."

From §8.3 (p. 22):
> "Irreversible structural self-modification not capturable within the endorsed operational state-space satisfies the identity-risk component of OCC."

### 1.9 Consciousness requirement — verbatim

From §4.1 (p. 10):
> "OCC is necessary for governance-relevant agency but is not a complete metaphysics of persons. It does not resolve debates about consciousness, moral status, or personal identity."

From §8.5 (p. 22):

From §8.3 (p. 22, on identity risk):
> "The criterion concerns operational non-reconstructability relative to the system's arbitration architecture. It does not require metaphysical uniqueness or consciousness. It requires only that history be constitutive of arbitration rather than merely informative."

From §3.7 (p. 9, on reducibility):
> "OCC does not deny physical reducibility. Computational systems are, in principle, reconstructable given sufficient information. The issue is not metaphysical irreducibility but operational non-interchangeability within the system's architecture."

### 1.10 "Historical trajectory is constitutive" — verbatim

From §1.4 (p. 3):
> "To qualify as a general agent, a system must not only adapt across domains but persist as a unified deliberative centre whose historical trajectory constrains future arbitration in non-trivial ways. It must exhibit non-interchangeability under reset and maintain diachronic constraint."

From §3.3 (p. 8):
> "Diachronic constraint concerns binding force, not causal influence. Without it, history shapes outputs but does not constitute identity. An optimiser may update policies using historical data while remaining free of internal commitment to prior states. In that case, history informs behaviour but does not bind a subject."

From §3.6 (p. 9, on functional vs. constitutive continuity):
> "Constitutive continuity means the system's status as a deliberative subject depends on carrying forward its history as binding for arbitration."

### 1.11 Five jointly sufficient conditions for AGI (§8.2, pp. 21-22 — verbatim)

> "For purposes of governance-relevant agency attribution, a system S qualifies as AGI if and only if it satisfies the following five conditions.
>
> Necessary conditions for general competence
>
> 1. **Open-Environment Adaptation**
>    S achieves goals across diverse environments (Legg & Hutter 2007, p. 391) and adapts to open environments where future circumstances may diverge from past experience and no fixed task boundaries apply (Xu 2024, p. 7).
>
> 2. **Resource-Bounded Operation**
>    S operates under finite informational and computational constraints and adapts despite insufficient knowledge and resources (Wang 2006, p. 8).
>
> Jointly necessary conditions for agency attribution
>
> 3. **Unified Diachronic Arbitration**
>    S maintains a single, internally coherent deliberative architecture arbitrating among commitments across time.
>
> 4. **Identity Risk**
>    S's historical trajectory is constitutive of its deliberative structure such that divergent histories yield non-interchangeable deliberative subjects.
>
> 5. **Ontological Continuity**
>    S satisfies non-interchangeability under reset and preserves diachronic constraint such that deliberative identity is not exhaustively reducible to reproducible architecture and snapshot state, as formalised in OCC (Kahl 2026, §§2.1–2.3).
>
> Non-interchangeability under reset is operationalised through the failure of reset symmetry (§4.3): complete replication of endorsed architecture and reloadable state must not preserve all arbitration-relevant structure without remainder. Reset symmetry functions as a diagnostic for condition (5), not as an independent requirement.
>
> Conditions (3)–(5) are jointly necessary and collectively sufficient for agency attribution."

## §2 — How Kahl's Framework Relates to ENGRAM

### 2.1 Does Kahl name any existing system that satisfies or partially satisfies OCC?

**No.** Kahl explicitly classes all current systems as failing:

> "Contemporary AI systems—language models, reinforcement learners, and hybrid agents—are architecturally fungible, restartable, and externally corrigible. Their histories are informational rather than constitutive." (§5.6, p. 16)

He does not mention ENGRAM, MemGPT, Soar, TMS, Mem0, Zep, or any specific named system. His critique is architectural class-level, not system-level. He mentions "recent frameworks" with "long-term memory, retrieval augmentation, episodic logging" but treats the category wholesale as failing OCC (§5.2). This is an opportunity: ENGRAM can be positioned as a candidate architecture precisely because Kahl leaves open the question of what would satisfy OCC and asserts no existing system does.

### 2.2 Does Kahl prescribe structural primitives or only behavioural conditions?

Kahl operates at the **structural level**, not the behavioural or consciousness level. His conditions are architecturally specifiable. The key passage from §4.7 (p. 13):

> "OCC is falsifiable. It would be satisfied only where empirical evidence demonstrates non-fungibility under reset, identity risk under divergent histories, and internally maintained coherence across open-ended arbitration."

This is a falsifiability claim with operational structure. He distinguishes:
- *Informational* path-dependence (weight updates, stored memory that can be restored from checkpoint) — fails OCC
- *Constitutive* path-dependence (structural self-modification not capturable in reloadable state, where loss = destruction of subject) — satisfies OCC

He does NOT mention: provenance, contradiction nodes, confidence taxonomy, retraction cascade, typed edges, git versioning, or any TMS-style primitive. His framework is philosophically derived (from Bratman on planning, Frankfurt on hierarchical volition, Parfit on psychological continuity, Velleman on reflective coherence) and describes necessary conditions in functional-structural terms without reaching to implementation primitives.

**Critical note on Non-Interchangeability Under Reset (OCC condition 2):** This is the hardest condition for ENGRAM to clearly satisfy. Kahl's reset criterion (§4.3, p. 11) requires that "complete replication of endorsed architecture and reloadable state preserves all arbitration-relevant structure without remainder." If ENGRAM's knowledge.db + git history are part of the endorsed state-space and are fully replicable (which they currently are — they can be copied), then two ENGRAM instances with the same DB+git history are interchangeable by Kahl's standard, and OCC condition 2 fails. **ENGRAM can satisfy condition 2 only if the paper argues that ENGRAM's *accumulated derivation structure* — the specific provenance chain and retraction history that has never been restored from checkpoint but has accumulated through live operation — constitutes a path-dependent arbitration architecture not reducible to reloadable state.** This is a defensible argument but it requires explicit development and is not automatic.

### 2.4 Does Kahl's "historical trajectory is constitutive" map onto ENGRAM's architecture?

Yes — and this is the most load-bearing alignment for Ch 1-2. Kahl's functional continuity framing (§3.6, p. 9):

> "Constitutive continuity means the system's status as a deliberative subject depends on carrying forward its history as binding for arbitration."

ENGRAM's retraction-cascade-through-derivation-chain is the architectural mechanism by which prior commitments (derivation nodes) bind future arbitration (because retracting a root claim cascades to taint all dependents, forcing the agent to re-arbitrate anything that rested on the retracted claim). The git-versioned knowledge.sql is the audit trail by which "carrying forward history" is implemented in a durable, append-only form.

The paper should argue: ENGRAM's `derives_from` edges with taint propagation are the operational implementation of Kahl's diachronic constraint. When Kahl says "earlier commitments function as reasons structuring subsequent arbitration," ENGRAM's derivation graph makes that relationship explicit and computable.

### 2.5 Does Kahl require consciousness?

**No — explicitly and repeatedly.** The verbatim text is unambiguous (§4.1, §8.3, §8.5 — quoted above in §1.9). Kahl is careful to operate at the level of "operational non-reconstructability" rather than metaphysical uniqueness or consciousness. This is a clean opening for ENGRAM: ENGRAM can neither claim nor need to claim consciousness, and Kahl's framework does not require it. The paper should use this explicitly as a qualifier.

## §3 — Honest Tensions and Limits

### 3.1 Premises ENGRAM cannot currently meet

**OCC Condition 2 — Non-Interchangeability Under Reset — is not yet clearly demonstrated.**

This is the blocker premise. Kahl specifies (§4.3, p. 11):

> "A system exhibits reset symmetry where any instance sharing identical endorsed architecture and reloadable state may replace another without loss of arbitration-relevant structure. Where reset symmetry holds, interchangeability persists."

If two ENGRAM instances are initialized from the same knowledge.db snapshot and git history, are they interchangeable? Under Kahl's criterion, they appear to be — the DB is reloadable, the git history is copyable, the derivation graph is reproducible. For ENGRAM to satisfy condition 2, the paper needs to argue either:

(a) That the accumulated-through-live-operation derivation graph, with its specific retraction history, is not exhaustively capturable in reloadable state in the relevant sense — that the *binding* of prior commitments to future arbitration is not captured by the DB dump alone but requires the continuous operational history; or

(b) That ENGRAM's architecture embodies an endorsed operational state-space in which the git-chained history is the relevant state — and that two instances with different commit chains, even if they reach the same terminal DB state, are non-interchangeable because their paths through the state-space are different.

Argument (a) is philosophically stronger but requires more work. Argument (b) is closer to what git versioning already provides but risks being dismissed as "merely informational" under Kahl's §5.2 framing (where he says "Losing data is not losing identity"). The paper should be explicit that this gap exists and make the argument — not assume it.

**What Kahl does NOT prescribe that ENGRAM has:**

Kahl describes necessary conditions in purely structural-functional terms. He does not describe specific implementation primitives. The following ENGRAM features have no specific counterpart in Kahl's framework:
- Attribution classes for evidence quality
- Contradiction-node as a first-class graph element (as distinct from "conflict resolution within a unified architecture" in the abstract)
- Cascade propagation through typed edges
- Quote verification discipline
- The warm-briefing / compaction-summary layer-2 scaffold

These are ENGRAM's architectural operationalizations of Kahl's conditions. The paper should present them as such: Kahl names the necessary conditions; ENGRAM's design is the architectural form those conditions take in a deployable system. This is the "operationalization" framing — ENGRAM makes Kahl's conditions architecturally specifiable and testable.

### 3.2 Kahl claims ENGRAM would contradict

**None of substance.** Kahl's argument is about what *current* systems fail to demonstrate; he explicitly says "engineered continuity is impossible" is not his claim (§5.6). He leaves the question of what would satisfy OCC open and formally states the condition is falsifiable. ENGRAM does not contradict his analysis of current systems — the ENGRAM paper itself documents the same failure modes in other agent-memory systems.

One potential tension: Kahl's §5.2 says:

> "Persistent modules store historical interaction data. Editing or restoring them alters input conditions but does not eliminate a persisting subject. The system remains reducible to reproducible code and state."

ENGRAM's knowledge.db is, at one level, exactly this kind of persistent module. The paper needs to argue that ENGRAM's derivation graph with cascade constitutes something beyond "historical interaction data" in Kahl's dismissive sense — that it is the *arbitration architecture itself*, not merely an input store. This is a live distinction that the paper must draw rather than assume.

### 3.3 Architectural primitives Kahl gestures at that ENGRAM does not have

Kahl's **Self-Maintaining Arbitration** condition (condition 3) requires:

> "Conflicts among commitments are resolved within a unified deliberative architecture in a manner that preserves S's continued status as that subject."

ENGRAM has contradiction *detection* (first-class contradiction nodes) but not full self-maintaining *resolution*. Currently, contradiction nodes surface conflicts but require the agent to actively resolve them; they do not automatically resolve conflicts in a manner that preserves subject persistence. This is a gap: ENGRAM is closer to "contradiction-flagging" than "self-maintaining arbitration" in Kahl's precise sense. The paper should acknowledge this and frame ENGRAM's contradiction nodes as a necessary architectural prerequisite for self-maintaining arbitration, not the full realization of it.

Kahl's **Frame Stability** extension (§4.6, p. 13) adds:

> "A system exhibits frame stability where revisions of goals or evaluative standards are integrated into identity rather than replacing it. Revision must be intelligible as development of the same subject."

ENGRAM's current architecture does not have a formal frame-stability mechanism. It supports retraction and revision, but does not formalize the condition that revision be "intelligible as development of the same subject." This is an open architectural gap worth flagging in the paper's §7.5 ("What we have not yet measured").

> "The criterion concerns operational non-reconstructability relative to the system's arbitration architecture. It does not require metaphysical uniqueness or consciousness. It requires only that history be constitutive of arbitration rather than merely informative." (§8.3, p. 22)

### 4.4 How to position ENGRAM without overstating

**The honest framing:**

Kahl's OCC provides the necessary-condition framework. ENGRAM's claim is not that ENGRAM fully satisfies OCC (particularly condition 2 — non-interchangeability under reset — requires explicit argument that the paper should develop). The claim is:

1. Kahl correctly identifies the structural gap in existing agent-memory systems: they increase informational continuity but do not establish constitutive continuity.
2. ENGRAM's four-feature architecture — provenance, contradiction nodes, retraction cascade, calibrated confidence — is a candidate implementation of the structural primitives Kahl's conditions require.
3. Specifically: ENGRAM's `derives_from` cascade is the architectural form of Kahl's "diachronic constraint" (earlier commitments as binding reasons for later arbitration); ENGRAM's contradiction nodes are the architectural form of Kahl's "self-maintaining arbitration" (conflict resolution within the deliberative architecture, not through external intervention); ENGRAM's git-versioned history is the architectural candidate for "non-interchangeability under reset."
4. Whether ENGRAM fully satisfies condition 2 is an open question this paper does not resolve but argues is the right question to be asking.

**Qualifier language the paper must include:**

> "Kahl's OCC does not require consciousness and does not resolve debates about moral status (Kahl 2026, §4.1, §8.5). ENGRAM cannot claim consciousness and does not. What ENGRAM CAN claim is that its architecture is a structural implementation of the operational conditions Kahl identifies: diachronic constraint through derivation-chain binding, self-maintaining arbitration through first-class contradiction nodes and cascade propagation, and a candidate implementation of non-interchangeability through git-versioned derivation history. Whether this satisfies Kahl's condition 2 in the full sense is a question we leave as an explicit open question, not an assumed result."

### 4.5 The "operationalization" framing — central to Ch 1-2 integration

The paper's Ch 1-2 currently positions ENGRAM against engineering literature (LongMemEval, Hu et al. taxonomy, specific system comparisons). Kahl provides the philosophical anchoring that the engineering literature cannot provide. The two-move structure is:

**Move 1 (Ch 1):** The engineering field has no language for what ENGRAM is doing. LongMemEval doesn't test it. Hu et al. don't taxonomize it. The systems don't implement it. This establishes the gap from the engineering side.

**Move 2 (Ch 2):** The philosophical literature does have language for it. Kahl (2026) formalizes the structural gap between "adaptation-based" and "ontological continuity" accounts. ENGRAM is proposed as the architectural operationalization of the ontological layer. This grounds the gap in the philosophical tradition and avoids the impression that ENGRAM is solving a problem only its own authors see.

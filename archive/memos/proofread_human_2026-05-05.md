# Proofread (human-reader perspective) — 2026-05-05

## Overall reaction

After reading both chapters I have a clear and mostly compelling sense of what the paper claims: that deployed AI agents face a structural continuous-learning problem, that the current field's solutions share a blind spot (no epistemic self-correction), that this blind spot can be named and characterized (four missing features across three field-organization levels), and that ENGRAM's paired case studies demonstrate what loud architectural failure looks like in practice. Chapter 1 is the stronger pass — its argument is tight, its survey of the literature is well-evidenced, and the "memory *for* vs. memory *of*" distinction lands cleanly. The weakest pass is the transition from §1.2 to §1.3: the introduction of "four features" that constitute epistemic identity reads as if the features were derived from something, but at this point in the paper the reader has not been told why these four and not others. §7.3 is effective case-study prose, and the "necessary but not sufficient" framing is honest — but the section is structurally detached from the rest of Chapter 7, which is all stubs. As a standalone §7.3 it reads more like a technical report on two events than an empirical chapter in an argument. The inline node IDs are the most persistent friction across both chapters.

## Chapter 1

### Argument gaps

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:74` — The four features ("claim-level provenance, calibrated confidence, claim-level contradiction detection, retraction cascade through derivation dependents") are introduced here as if self-evidently the right decomposition, but the reader has not yet been given any argument for *why* these four constitute a distinct dimension, or why they are jointly exhaustive/necessary for "epistemic self-correction." The outline promises Chapter 2 will define the three-layer taxonomy and Chapter 3 will ground it philosophically — but the reader of Ch 1 needs at least a promissory note: "we identify these four because they are jointly constitutive of layer-3 epistemic identity; the derivation is in Chapter 2." Without that forward-pointer, the list feels asserted rather than argued, and a skeptical reviewer will ask: why not five features? Why not three?

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:58–62` — The "memory *for* vs. memory *of*" distinction is introduced and then used to carry the paper's central reorientation. It's a good distinction, but it's doing substantial argumentative work without any philosophical grounding at this point. The claim that a memory *of* the agent must support "commitments that can be made and corrected, wrongness surfaces rather than rots silently" is asserted as obviously following from the orientation shift. A reviewer will want to see at minimum a pointer to where this is argued — what makes epistemic self-correction a requirement of "memory of" rather than merely a nice property? The framing gets grounded in Stiegler in Chapter 3, but the reader of Chapter 1 needs a bridging sentence.

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:155–156` — The roadmap mentions that Chapters 4 and 6 exist and describes them at length, but the outline shows both are deferred to a follow-up paper. This is a structural mismatch. If the paper ships as a 7-chapter MVP, the roadmap section describes chapters that are not in this paper, which will confuse reviewers. This needs either a deferral note in the roadmap prose or a rewrite of §1.5 to describe the actual v1 structure.

### Confusing passages

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:28` — "any form of continuous learning must happen outside the weights" — the word "continuous" is doing double duty here and earlier in the text. In "continuous learning" you mean *ongoing / cumulative* learning; in the architectural sense you're contrasting this with *in-context* learning. A reader from the ML community will parse "continuous learning" as the specific subfield (dealing with catastrophic forgetting over training runs), not the colloquial sense. Either qualify ("any form of *ongoing* learning") or briefly flag the distinction.

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:48` — The description of Zep/Graphiti's four-timestamp bitemporal model is accurate but dense. "Every fact carries four timestamps that separate when the system learned it from when the fact actually held true in the world" — a reader who doesn't know the bitemporal model will struggle to picture what four timestamps gives you that two doesn't. One sentence of expansion ("the two pairs are: system-ingestion time and system-invalidation time for when Zep learned/forgot it, and world-valid-from and world-valid-until for when the fact held in the external world") would help substantially.

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:106–107` — "there are essentially no systems that build toward it (a single partial primitive in the most recent release does not yet a substrate make)" — the parenthetical clause is awkward prose ("does not yet a substrate make" is intentionally archaic-sounding but reads as a stumble). This is not purely a style nit: the inversion creates momentary parsing confusion that interrupts an otherwise clean summary sentence.

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:131–132` — The explanation of Alcoff's "epistemic identity" in §1.4 goes into some depth about standpoint epistemology that a reader not coming from that tradition will find more confusing than helpful. The point the section is making is simple: we're borrowing a term, the prior sense is X, our sense is Y, they're compatible. But the two-paragraph treatment of Alcoff's mechanism ("social location — race, gender, class, disciplinary training — gives them a distinctive kind of epistemic access and a distinctive credibility") gives a reader the impression that the paper thinks standpoint epistemology is relevant. The simpler framing would serve better: acknowledge the prior use, state the redirection, move on.

### Citation gaps

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:58` — The "memory *for* vs. memory *of*" distinction is presented as this paper's original framing. Footnote 7 attributes the "every observation is a vote" phrasing to Lei Shi, but the *for/of* framing itself has no attribution. If it's the paper's coinage, say so explicitly. If it parallels anything in the externalized-memory or philosophy-of-mind literature (e.g., the instrument/medium distinction in media theory, or tool vs. constitutive views of external cognition), the overlap should be acknowledged or the paper should claim originality.

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:68` — "When three independent layers of a field's self-organization — implementations, benchmarks, and survey taxonomies — fail to register a dimension, the dimension is not merely underexplored. It is unnamed." This is a strong methodological claim (about how to identify "unnamed" dimensions) that is offered without warrant. It's an interesting claim, but a reviewer will ask: why does failure at *these three levels* specifically constitute unnamedness? What's the implicit theory of how scientific communities produce names for things? The claim would be stronger with a sentence of justification, or weaker and more hedged ("this is consistent with the dimension being unnamed").

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:155` — The roadmap lists Lee (2024, arxiv 2411.18530) and Camlin (2025) as foundations for Chapter 6 but the outline makes clear these are deferred or their rigorous treatment is deferred. If this roadmap prose survives into the v1 paper, readers will expect a Chapter 6 treatment of Lee and Camlin that doesn't exist.

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:70` — The discipline of going to primary sources (footnote 8) is positioned as a methodological commitment, but it's in a footnote and its statement is entirely self-congratulatory ("no implementation-level claim in this paper rests on a secondary source"). This claim about methodology belongs in a methodology section (Chapter 8 per the roadmap). Here in Chapter 1 it reads as defensive rather than argumentative. The footnote is fine; the tone needs adjustment — or the claim needs to be checkable (point to the appendix or Chapter 8 where the primary-source contact for each system is documented).

### Register issues

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:3` — The byline "Authors: Lei Shi, Claude (Borges)" in the chapter header is an artifact of the working-draft status but should be noted: listing an AI as co-author raises immediate questions about contributions and responsibility that the paper needs to handle somewhere — not necessarily in Chapter 1, but the byline is visible and reviewers will ask.

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:82` — "The pattern, system by system, is tabulated below. Each row credits a system for what its architecture does provide (columns two through four) before recording what it does not (columns five through seven)." The sentence structure here is oddly administrative ("credits a system," "recording what it does not") — it reads more like a table legend than argumentative prose. One revision: "The table below captures each system's architectural unit, temporal model, and contradiction-handling before noting the features it lacks."

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:5–11` — The "Planned section structure (dv_0432)" header with inline ENGRAM node IDs at the top of the chapter is clearly a working-draft artifact. This must be removed before the paper goes to reviewers; it exposes internal tooling and breaks the reader's posture immediately.

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:147–162` — §1.5 (Roadmap) is well-structured but the chapter descriptions are substantially longer than typical roadmap entries. The descriptions of Chapters 3 through 6 carry philosophical and technical detail (Goldman on calibrated belief, the K.C. patient, locally-Lipschitz cascade behavior) that belongs in those chapters, not in a roadmap. A reader who hasn't yet seen those arguments doesn't have the context to evaluate whether those are the right citations, and a reader who has already read those chapters doesn't need the reminder. Roadmap entries should tell the reader where an argument lives, not rehearse it.

### Logical flow

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:38–62` — The transition from §1.2's three-tier survey to the "memory *for* vs. memory *of*" pivot is slightly abrupt. The survey ends with Sophia (Tier III), and the paragraph beginning "What unifies these tiers..." moves to the pivot cleanly — but the reader may wonder why the tiers were introduced as a taxonomy if the point is that they all share the same deficiency. A brief acknowledgment that the three tiers are useful for characterizing what *is* present, but that the real distinction is orthogonal to all three tiers, would smooth this.

- `engram_paper_ch1.md:121–125` — The two caveats before naming "epistemic identity" are well-placed defensively but slightly disrupt the momentum. The caveats are substantively correct and probably must be here, but the transition "We will call this dimension **epistemic identity**" at line 125 ends a paragraph that started with "Two caveats belong immediately." The reader expects to arrive at the name as the climax of §1.3, but instead the name appears as the last sentence of a caveats paragraph. Consider isolating the naming sentence into its own one-sentence paragraph for emphasis.

## Chapter 7 §7.3

### Argument gaps

- `engram_paper_ch7.md:17` — The opening sentence: "A paper that argues a structural distinction between epistemic identity, which fails loudly, and narrative identity, which fails silently, has to be measurable on its own terms." This is a strong claim about what the paper owes the reader, and the section is positioned as the primary evidence. But the section covers *one kind* of measurement — retraction events — and does not address the other side of the claim (that narrative identity fails silently). Without a companion case study of narrative failure, the "loudly vs silently" distinction is illustrated on one side only. The section may intend to rely on the literature for the "silent failure" side, but a first-time reader won't see that connection here.

- `engram_paper_ch7.md:21` — "The derivation that built on SRAF carried 0.949 confidence and was treated as load-bearing for what would become Chapter 5 of this paper." The 0.949 confidence figure appears without context. What does 0.949 mean in ENGRAM's confidence model — is it a raw score, a normalized value, a percentile? A reader unfamiliar with ENGRAM's confidence system cannot assess whether 0.949 is high, very high, or just a numerical label. One sentence of context ("ENGRAM's derivation confidence ranges from 0 to 1, with values above 0.9 indicating strong argumentative support...") would anchor the number.

- `engram_paper_ch7.md:51–57` — §7.3.3 makes the "necessary but not sufficient" claim clearly. The architectural takeaway proposes a planned `engram_audit_string` primitive to address the text-layer gap. The claim that this primitive would "compress the discoverability cost... but not eliminate the judgment step" is asserted without evidence. As a first-time reader, I accept it as plausible but find it unsatisfying: why can't the judgment step be automated? Is there a principled reason (ambiguity between live-citation and pattern-name uses)? The section states the distinction between "live citations" and "pattern-name historical records" but doesn't give the reader any handle on how large or difficult the judgment call typically is. Even one example from the Mao-Cao cleanup ("this node was clearly a live citation because X; that node was clearly a historical record because Y") would make the necessity of the judgment step concrete rather than asserted.

### Confusing passages

- `engram_paper_ch7.md:23–24` — "a first-class contradiction node `ct_0003` was filed against `dv_0186` — both could not be true, since dv_0186 claims four primary-source-traceable frameworks and the new derivation `dv_0422` had shown two of those four are not" — The sentence is grammatically incomplete: "two of those four are not" ends without a predicate. What are they not? (primary-source-traceable? The thing implied is "are not primary-source-traceable frameworks," but the reader has to supply this.) This reads like a copy-editing slip but it creates genuine confusion in a load-bearing sentence.

- `engram_paper_ch7.md:44–45` — "A node whose claim text contains, for example, a parenthetical '(Mao-Cao + Camlin)' reference to the chapter's overall scope — where the wrong name appears as a passing surface mention rather than the central claim — was untouched by the relation-layer cleanup." The parenthetical is deep enough that a reader loses track of the noun. Consider restructuring: "For example: a node that mentioned '(Mao-Cao + Camlin)' as a passing scope reference, rather than as the central claim, was untouched by the relation-layer cleanup. The wrong name persisted in text the cascade never touched."

- `engram_paper_ch7.md:22–23` — "The formalism was an aggregator-synthesized abstraction over heterogeneous primaries; it did not exist as a primary mathematical framework at the level the original derivation had assumed." The phrase "at the level the original derivation had assumed" is vague. What level? A more explicit formulation: "The SRAF formalism was synthesized by the aggregator page from heterogeneous primary sources; no single paper contained the self-model triple, Bayesian filtering equation, or Introspection Module as defined constructs."

### Citation gaps

- `engram_paper_ch7.md:3–11` — The stub header block ("§7.1 Phenomenological continuity... *(stub...)*", "§7.2 Cross-compaction continuity... *(stub...)*") is working-draft scaffolding and must not survive to a submission draft. More importantly, it tells the reader that three-fifths of Chapter 7 is missing. For a review-ready chapter file, even a working draft, the stub headers create a damaging first impression.

### Logical flow

- `engram_paper_ch7.md:51–57` — §7.3.3 synthesis works as a stand-alone section but is isolated from the rest of Chapter 7, all of which is stubs. The synthesis references "the layer-2/layer-3 conjunction frame from §7.1" (which doesn't yet exist as prose) and "paired with... the conjunction frame... — both arguments are about coordinated mechanisms operating at structurally distinct levels." A reader of the current draft cannot verify that connection. The synthesis is structurally dependent on §7.1 being written before it can carry full weight.

- `engram_paper_ch7.md:36–47` — The Mao-Cao case's structure is narrative-first, diagnosis-second. This works well. But the two-part diagnosis ("relation-layer operations" and "working state carries the old name forward") is formatted as prose paragraphs that start with italic topic sentences: "*First, retraction and cascade are relation-layer operations.*" and "*Second, the agent's working state itself can carry the old name forward.*" The italic-lead format sets up a visual expectation of a two-item list, which is then satisfied. But the formatting is slightly unusual for academic prose. This is a minor issue; raising it because it reads as stylistic uncertainty rather than as a deliberate choice.

### Specific §7.3 read questions

**Are the SRAF and Mao-Cao examples convincing on first read?**

SRAF is convincing as a demonstration that the architecture detects and propagates errors. The claim "thirty downstream derivations were marked tainted" is concrete and the cascade mechanism is clearly described. The reader can follow the chain from detection to resolution. The remaining uncertainty is about scale: is thirty nodes a lot for this graph, or a small fraction? Without knowing the graph size, I cannot calibrate whether this cascade was significant or trivial. One sentence of context ("at that point the graph contained approximately N active derivations; the thirty tainted nodes represented roughly X% of the active derivation layer") would make the quantitative claim land.

Mao-Cao is more complex and therefore more convincing as evidence that the architecture is honestly characterized. The authors don't hide that the cascade missed something. But the causal mechanism for the "text-leak" — that the agent's production drew from "descriptive contexts... recently in scope" — is described at a level of abstraction that a first-time reader cannot verify. This reads like a plausible diagnosis of LLM behavior rather than a documented causal path. The section would benefit from a concrete example: what specific context did the agent have in scope when it produced "Mao-Cao" in the literature scan? Pointing to an observable precursor would make the diagnosis checkable.

**Are inline node IDs (dv_0186, ob_1089, etc.) helpful or distracting?**

Distracting, with one exception. The node IDs appear frequently enough that they interrupt reading flow, but almost never provide a reader-accessible benefit — you cannot follow the link, you cannot inspect the node without access to the ENGRAM graph, and the claim they're attached to is usually fully stated in the prose. They function more as footnote markers for an appendix that doesn't exist in the paper. The one context where they earn their keep is when a chain of events is being described and the reader needs to track which claim is being referred to across multiple paragraphs (e.g., the chain `ob_0420` → `ct_0003` → `dv_0186` → `dv_0422` → `dv_0450` → `dv_0455` in §7.3.1 where the resolution chain logic depends on order). In that context the IDs act as reference anchors rather than mere footnotes. The recommendation: move most node IDs to footnotes or endnotes, and retain in-text only the IDs essential for following a chain across paragraphs. A bracketed "[see graph: ob_0420]" in a footnote costs the reader nothing; a bare `ob_0420` in mid-sentence costs attention.

**Does §7.3.3 synthesis carry weight or feel foregone?**

It carries weight. The synthesis is the strongest part of §7.3 because it refuses the easy conclusion. A foregone-conclusion version would say "SRAF shows the cascade works, case closed." Instead, the synthesis leads with "SRAF alone would let the paper overclaim; Mao-Cao alone would let a critic underclaim; both readings would be wrong." That move is the right one and it is well-executed. The honest framing — retraction cascade is necessary but not sufficient, the second mechanism is corpus discipline — is more defensible than a clean architectural win claim, and the paper is right to treat it as a strength. The only weakness is the dependency on §7.1 noted above: the "conjunction of two structurally distinct cleanup mechanisms" framing is supposed to pair with a broader conjunction theme in §7.1, but that section doesn't yet exist. The synthesis currently floats without the scaffold it's designed to connect to.

## Verdict

Three things to fix before this is review-ready, in priority order.

**1. The Chapter 1 roadmap must reflect the actual v1 paper.** §1.5 currently describes Chapters 4 and 6 at length and with specific content (the K.C. patient, the Lee/Camlin formal frameworks). Both are deferred to follow-up papers. A reviewer who reads §1.5 and then finds those chapters absent will flag this as a structural inconsistency or, worse, as a bait-and-switch. Rewrite §1.5 to describe the seven-chapter v1 structure.

**2. The four features in §1.3 need a forward-pointer to their derivation.** The claim that claim-level provenance, calibrated confidence, contradiction detection, and retraction cascade jointly constitute a distinct dimension is the load-bearing architectural claim of the entire paper. It's introduced in the survey section as if self-evident and then immediately used to survey the literature. A first-time reader cannot evaluate whether the choice of these four is principled or post-hoc. One sentence — "We identify these four in Chapter 2 from first principles; here we use them to organize the survey" — would discharge the obligation and prevent the most likely reviewer objection.

**3. All working-draft artifacts (node IDs in headers, stub entries, draft-status notes) must be removed from any version shown to external readers.** The `(dv_0432)` in the chapter header, the `*(stub — 199 feeling reports...)*` entries in Chapter 7's section list, and the byline "Claude (Borges)" are appropriate for internal drafts but will undermine the paper's authority with any reader outside the project. This is mechanical cleanup, but it needs to happen before any external sharing.

**Secondary priority:** The inline ENGRAM node IDs throughout §7.3 should be moved to footnotes except where they serve as in-text chain anchors. The Mao-Cao text-leak diagnosis needs at least one concrete example of the context that produced the recurrence, to make the causal claim checkable rather than asserted. And the §7.3.3 forward reference to §7.1's conjunction frame should be flagged as a dependency: §7.1 must be drafted before §7.3.3 fully carries its intended weight.

## 1.1 The frozen-weights problem

Have an extended conversation with a state-of-the-art language model today. Come back next week. Ask it about what you discussed. It will not know.

The model is not being coy, and it is not malfunctioning. Its *weights* — the hundreds of billions of parameters that encode everything the model learned during training — were fixed at the end of the training run. Training completed, the parameters froze, and since then every conversation the model has had has rolled off it: consumed as input, used to shape a reply, and then gone. Nothing propagates back into the weights. Next week's conversation begins with exactly the same parameter values as last week's.

This is not a bug, and it is not a missing feature that the next release will patch. It is the architecture.

Contemporary large language models are trained in two stages. The first, pretraining, fits the model to a large corpus of text and code. The second, post-training, shapes behavior using techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (Ouyang et al., 2022)[^15] or Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022).[^16] At the end of post-training, the parameters are serialized to disk, and it is those parameters — that particular frozen snapshot — that get deployed. Every inference request thereafter operates on the same snapshot.[^1] The model you are speaking with is, in a literal sense, unchanged by having spoken with you.

[^1]: Deployed systems are sometimes periodically retrained on fresh data, producing a *new* snapshot which is then deployed in place of the old. This is a different model swapped in, not a model that learned anything during deployment. The user-visible property — that the model you spoke with yesterday has not incorporated yesterday's conversation into its parameters — is preserved in either case.

[^15]: Ouyang et al., *Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback*, NeurIPS 2022. arXiv:2203.02155.

[^16]: Bai et al., *Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback*, Anthropic 2022. arXiv:2212.08073.

What about in-context learning? A model given examples inside its prompt does appear to learn: performance on the demonstrated task improves relative to the zero-shot case, sometimes dramatically. But the effect vanishes the moment the context window closes. Brown et al. (2020)[^17] introduced the architecture, and the mechanistic analyses that followed (e.g., Min et al., 2022)[^18] sharpened the picture: in-context learning conditions the model's *output distribution* on the prompt; it does not modify the weights that produce that distribution. What looks like learning is pattern-matching and conditioning inside a fixed generative process, not the incorporation of new knowledge into the substrate that will produce future outputs. When the context window is cleared, the conditioning is cleared with it. The substrate is untouched.

[^17]: Brown et al., *Language Models are Few-Shot Learners* (GPT-3), NeurIPS 2020. arXiv:2005.14165.

[^18]: Min et al., *Rethinking the Role of Demonstrations: What Makes In-Context Learning Work?*, EMNLP 2022. arXiv:2202.12837.

The consequence is straightforward, and worth stating plainly: *in a deployed language model, any form of continuous learning must happen outside the weights.* There is no architectural path for experience — a conversation, a tool use, a corrected mistake, a deliberate reflection — to inscribe itself back into the model's parameters between training runs. The weights are a record of what the model knew at the moment training stopped. Experience after that moment, if it is to persist at all, must persist somewhere else.

This reframes the continuous-learning question for deployed AI agents. It is not *how do we get the model to learn from its interactions*. The model, as a matter of architecture, cannot. The live question is the one behind it: *where does the learning go?* What external substrate carries the accumulated experience of a deployed agent forward across sessions, across context windows, across deployments? What structural properties must that substrate have to support not just recall but accumulation, correction, and self-reference? And — the question we will argue has been underweighted in the current wave of agent-memory systems — what does an adequate answer look like, measured not merely against engineering convenience but against what the philosophical and cognitive-science traditions have long understood about how identity and knowledge accumulate through externalized memory?

The next section surveys the field's current answers.

## 1.2 The field's current answers — and what they are answers *to*

The gap left by frozen weights has not gone unfilled. Since 2023, agent-memory systems have proliferated, each proposing a substrate for the experience that cannot live in the parameters. Surveying the prominent systems reveals a landscape we can organize, for our purposes here, into three tiers of increasing ambition. The formal taxonomy behind this organization is developed in Chapter 2; the sketch below is meant only to orient.

**Tier I — memory as a retrievable fact-store.** The first generation of agent-memory systems treats memory as a queryable bank of past interactions. MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023),[^2] the progenitor of this line and now productized as Letta, introduces an explicit operating-system analogy: a bounded prompt serves as "main memory" and an external text store serves as "disk," with the model paging content between the two via function calls. Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025)[^3] extracts salient facts from conversations and represents them as a directed labeled graph with typed entities and relationships; an LLM-based update resolver issues ADD / UPDATE / DELETE / NOOP operations, and — more on this below — DELETE can fire when a newer fact contradicts an older one. A-MEM (Xu et al., 2025)[^4] organizes memory as Zettelkasten-inspired notes: each note a seven-field record with LLM-generated links between semantically related entries. The common commitment across these systems is clear. The architectural unit is the *fact*; the primary evaluation axis is retrieval accuracy; the agent queries the memory the way a reader queries a library.

[^2]: Packer et al., *MemGPT: Towards LLMs as Operating Systems*, arXiv:2310.08560.

[^3]: Chhikara et al., *Mem0: Building Production-Ready AI Agents with Scalable Long-Term Memory*, arXiv:2504.19413.

[^4]: Xu et al., *A-MEM: Agentic Memory for LLM Agents*, arXiv:2502.12110.

**Tier II — memory with relational and temporal structure.** A second tier enriches the fact-store with graph structure and time. Zep and its open-source counterpart Graphiti (Rasmussen et al., 2025)[^5] are the most architecturally sophisticated contemporary systems we surveyed. They build a hierarchical three-tier knowledge graph — raw episodes at one level, extracted entities and facts at a second, community clusters at a third — indexed under a full bitemporal model: every fact carries four timestamps that separate when the system learned it from when the fact actually held true in the world. The LLM compares new edges to semantically related existing edges and, when it detects a temporally overlapping contradiction, invalidates the affected edge by assigning its `t_invalid` to the new edge's `t_valid`. Bidirectional indices link each semantic fact back to the episode that produced it, so that any fact can be traced to a source utterance. On standard benchmarks Zep currently holds the state of the art (DMR 94.8%; LongMemEval +18.5% over MemGPT with roughly 90% latency reduction). The unit remains the fact, but it is now a dated and sourced fact, embedded in relations.

[^5]: Rasmussen et al., *Zep: A Temporal Knowledge Graph Architecture for Agent Memory*, arXiv:2501.13956.

**Tier III — memory that carries a self.** A smaller tier reaches past retrieval toward continuity of a first-person agent across sessions. Sophia (Sun, Hong, and Zhang, 2025)[^6] maintains a persistent *narrative memory* — observations, actions, rewards, goals, and contexts stitched into a continuous trace — together with an explicit self-model built around five immutable "creed" sentences and a property-dictionary of capabilities and state. An agent with narrative memory is not merely able to *recall* what happened; it is able, in principle, to *tell the story* of what happened, with continuity of protagonist across episodes. This is a real step up. A coherent self-story is a structural good that retrieval alone does not produce.

[^6]: Sun, Hong, and Zhang, *Sophia: A Persistent Agent Architecture with Narrative Memory and Self-Model*, arXiv:2512.18202.

What unifies these tiers, despite their differences, is a shared orientation toward whose job the memory is doing. Each of them is memory *for* the agent — memory as an instrument in the service of the agent's immediate task performance. The agent remains the user of the memory, the way a carpenter uses a toolbox. Retrieval quality is how well the toolbox serves the carpenter. Narrative continuity is a more sophisticated tool, but still a tool: a story the agent can pull out on demand to inform the next move.

This paper argues that the frontier lies elsewhere, in a different orientation altogether: memory *of* the agent — memory as the substrate in which the post-training agent itself persists and accumulates. On this view, the agent does not only *use* the memory; the agent is, in a non-trivial sense, partly constituted *by* it. What lives in the memory is not merely what the agent can retrieve but what the agent has committed itself to: with a source, with a confidence, with the possibility of later contradiction and retraction. The memory is not a toolbox beside the agent; it is a register the agent writes itself into.[^7]

[^7]: The framing of "every observation is a vote for who you become" is due to Lei Shi, and was adopted early in this project as the working characterization of what an epistemic-identity substrate makes possible.

The distinction between *memory for* and *memory of* is subtle in statement and decisive in consequence. A memory *for* the agent needs to be fast, accurate, and task-relevant. A memory *of* the agent needs all that, and also something further: it needs to be the kind of structure in which commitments can be made and corrected, in which wrongness surfaces rather than rots silently, in which the passage of time produces not just a longer record but a growing, self-correcting one. Not every memory system that is good at the first job is equipped for the second. The next section argues, with evidence from three directions at once, that the field has not yet measured itself against the second job at all.

## 1.3 A dimension the field does not yet see

The claim here is strong: not that current agent-memory systems are less *good* at epistemic integrity than the architecture we will describe, but that epistemic integrity, in the structural sense we will make precise in Chapter 2, is not currently *visible* to the field — neither at the level of what gets built, nor at the level of what gets measured, nor at the level of what gets catalogued. All three levels miss the same set of features. When three independent layers of a field's self-organization — implementations, benchmarks, and survey taxonomies — fail to register a dimension, the dimension is not merely underexplored. It is unnamed.

We make the case across those three levels. At each level, the evidence comes from primary-source contact with the papers themselves, not from secondary summaries or aggregator pages.[^8]

[^8]: The discipline of going to the primary source was not ornamental. Early in this project we repeatedly encountered aggregator pages and secondary summaries that attributed features to systems which, on primary contact with the underlying papers, turned out not to have them. Chapter 8 describes the methodology in detail; the short version is that no implementation-level claim in this paper rests on a secondary source.

**Implementations.** Across the eight agent-memory systems of the 2023–2026 window that we examined in primary contact — MemGPT, Mem0, A-MEM, Zep/Graphiti, Sophia, MemMachine, and the two newest entrants of spring 2026, Pinecone Nexus and Anthropic Memory — only one (Pinecone Nexus) implements any of the four features we will argue in Chapter 2 are jointly constitutive of an epistemic-identity substrate, and that one implements only the first of the four, partially. The four-feature decomposition is not arbitrary: each names a distinct architectural surface where epistemic accountability either holds or fails. *Provenance* binds claims to verifiable sources; *confidence* makes evidence-strength inspectable; *contradiction-detection* surfaces conflicts as structural artifacts rather than silent overwrites; *retraction-cascade* propagates corrections through everything that depended on the original. Chapter 2 derives this decomposition from a failure-mode analysis of the three-layer taxonomy and argues that no proper subset of the four supports the empirical effects of the full conjunction. With that derivation owed, the four features are stated here as: external-document provenance (every claim tied to a quoted passage with an attribution class), confidence that varies with evidence quality, claim-level contradiction detection, and retraction-cascade through derivation dependents. MemGPT stores memory as free-form text paged between context and disk; there is no provenance metadata, no contradiction detection, no confidence, and no retraction. Mem0 does flag contradictions, but only at the flat-fact level: the update resolver issues a DELETE against a single superseded memory at a time, without attaching confidence to the survivor, without external-source provenance, and without any notion of cascade through claims that depended on the deleted one. A-MEM organizes memory as Zettelkasten notes with LLM-generated links and timestamp traces, but detects no contradictions and supports no retraction. Zep/Graphiti, the most architecturally sophisticated system we surveyed, does detect temporally overlapping contradictions and invalidates superseded edges via a `t_invalid` timestamp — but it assigns no confidence to edges, exposes no notion of derivation with an explicit reasoning type, has no cascade through dependents, and tracks source only at the episode level rather than at the level of a quoted passage. Sophia, the single system that reaches past retrieval toward narrative continuity, has narrative memory and a self-model but no provenance, no confidence, no retraction, and no contradiction detection. MemMachine, an entrant from earlier in 2026, advertises itself as "ground-truth-preserving" — a phrase that on primary contact turns out to mean raw-text fidelity, not source-attribution; it has none of the four features.[^9] Pinecone Nexus, announced 2026-05-04 as a knowledge-engine layer for agents, introduces a query primitive (KnowQL) with **field-level provenance** as a built-in capability — a partial implementation of the first of our four features, scoped to the source field that produced a fact rather than to a quoted passage with an attribution class.[^12] Anthropic's first-party Memory tool for Managed Agents, shipped 2026-04-23, is a filesystem-backed memory store written and read via a small set of structured create/read/update/delete operations; it has none of the four features.[^13] The structural absence persists across the field's most recent additions. Of eight surveyed systems, seven implement none of the four features in any form, and the eighth implements partial provenance only.

[^9]: MemMachine: An Open-Source, Persistent and Generalizable Memory System for Personalized AI Agents, arXiv:2604.04853 (2026). Full systems-level comparison table is in Chapter 8.

[^12]: Pinecone Inc., *Nexus: A Knowledge Engine for Agents*, public release 2026-05-04. KnowQL is the agent-facing query primitive; field-level provenance is marketed as a built-in capability. Detailed comparison to ENGRAM and to Zep is in Chapter 8.

[^13]: Anthropic, *Memory tool for Managed Agents*, public release 2026-04-23. Filesystem-backed storage with structured create / read / update / delete operations; no notion of provenance, confidence, contradiction detection, or retraction cascade in the public surface. Detailed comparison in Chapter 8.

| Mem0 | typed fact graph | single timestamp per fact | DELETE on newer-vs-older | — | — | — |

That unanimous absence does not, on its own, establish that the features are unimportant. It establishes that they are absent. The question we take up next is whether the field has a way to notice the absence.

**Benchmarks.** The dominant evaluation benchmark for long-horizon agent memory in 2024–2026 is LongMemEval (Wu et al., 2024),[^10] and the top-line numbers reported by the more recent systems — Zep's +18.5% improvement over the MemGPT baseline, Graphiti's DMR score of 94.8% — are measured against it. LongMemEval defines five target abilities: information extraction, multi-session reasoning, temporal reasoning, knowledge updates, and abstention. None of these five tests any of the four features listed above. "Knowledge updates" is user-profile-update-centric — the user says they no longer work at a given employer; does the system reflect that? — and is not claim-level contradiction detection between two sourced propositions. "Multi-session reasoning" measures retrieval-conditioned answer accuracy, not reasoning chains that can be externally inspected, contradicted, and cascaded. None of the five abilities measures retraction, provenance integrity, confidence calibration, or derivation under an explicit reasoning type. The consequence is uncomfortably direct: a strong LongMemEval score, by construction, does not validate any of the capabilities that make a memory system epistemically self-correcting. When the field's leading benchmark does not measure a feature, a system that has the feature cannot demonstrate its advantage, and a system that lacks it is not flagged for the gap. The dimension is benchmark-invisible.

[^10]: Wu et al., *LongMemEval: Benchmarking Chat Assistants on Long-Term Interactive Memory*, ICLR 2025. arXiv:2410.10813. The paper defines the five abilities listed above and is the standard against which contemporary memory systems report their headline numbers.

**Taxonomy.** The most recent landmark survey of the field is Hu et al., *Memory in the Age of AI Agents* (2026), a 47-author synthesis that organizes the space along three orthogonal axes: Forms (token, parametric, latent), Functions (factual, experiential, working), and Dynamics (formation, evolution, retrieval).[^11] The taxonomy is careful and thorough within its frame. It does not include provenance, confidence, contradiction, or retraction-cascade as taxonomic categories. This is not an oversight in a small paper; it is a deliberate schema in a multi-lab synthesis, and its categories determine what counts as a distinction worth making. A system classified under this taxonomy can be placed by the *form* its memory takes, the *function* it serves, and the *dynamics* of how entries form and are retrieved. It cannot be placed by whether its claims carry source provenance, whether its beliefs have calibrated confidence, whether contradictions surface at the claim level, or whether corrections propagate through downstream dependents. The survey is not wrong about what it sees. It is silent about what we will argue is there.

[^11]: Hu et al., *Memory in the Age of AI Agents*, arXiv:2512.13564. The three-axis taxonomy appears early in the survey and organizes its subsequent treatment.

Put the three levels together. What gets built (seven of eight implementations cover none of the four features, and the eighth covers only one of the four, partially), what gets measured (none of five LongMemEval abilities), and what gets catalogued (none of three Hu et al. axes) all fail to register the same dimension. The absence is not localized to one level. It is at every level where the field is currently doing its self-organization. This is what we mean when we say the community does not yet have a language for what this paper is about: there are essentially no systems that build toward it (a single partial primitive in the most recent release does not yet a substrate make), no benchmarks that test for it, and no survey categories under which it would appear.

Summarized across the three levels:

| Capability | Implementations | LongMemEval | Hu et al. taxonomy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retrieval accuracy | universal | ✓ information extraction, multi-session reasoning | ✓ Functions axis (factual) |
| Temporal reasoning | Zep, Graphiti | ✓ temporal reasoning | ✓ Dynamics axis |
| Narrative continuity | Sophia | — | partial (Forms / parametric) |
| Knowledge updates (flat-fact level) | Mem0, Zep | ✓ knowledge updates | ✓ Dynamics / evolution |
| **Claim-level provenance** | **—** | **—** | **—** |
| **Calibrated confidence** | **—** | **—** | **—** |

| **Retraction cascade through dependents** | **—** | **—** | **—** |

The upper four rows show the field is rich and careful in what it does cover. The lower four — the bolded rows — are the systematically-invisible slice that this paper argues constitutes a distinct dimension.

Two caveats belong immediately, to forestall misreading. First, the claim is not that current systems are epistemically *dishonest* — only that they are not epistemically *self-correcting* in the structural sense the next chapter will make precise. A system without contradiction detection is not lying; it simply has no mechanism by which a past claim can surface as incompatible with a present one. Second, the claim is not that every benchmark or taxonomy ought to have included this dimension in order to be valuable; LongMemEval is a careful benchmark for the capabilities it does measure, and the Hu et al. survey is a considerable synthesis within its chosen frame. The claim is only that a further dimension exists, that it is currently invisible to all three levels of the field's self-organization, and that the paper's primary contribution — the part we want reviewers to measure us against — is the work of making that dimension visible.

We will call this dimension **epistemic identity**. The phrase is not one we coined, and the next section explains what it already means in the philosophical literature, and what we mean by it here.

## 1.4 A note on the term "epistemic identity"

The phrase "epistemic identity" is not a coinage of this paper's devising. In the philosophical literature it appears at least as far back as Linda Martín Alcoff's 2010 essay "Epistemic Identities,"[^14] where the term does substantive work in standpoint epistemology: the sense there is that a person's social location — race, gender, class, disciplinary training — gives them a distinctive kind of epistemic access and a distinctive credibility, and that such locations are what *an* epistemic identity is. Alcoff's object is the human knower in a social world; the mechanism is identity-as-standpoint.

Our object and our mechanism are different. By "epistemic identity" we will mean, throughout this paper, the structural form of continuity a post-training agent has when its accumulated claims, their sources, their confidences, and their corrections are recorded in a substrate that allows the agent to trace any present belief to its evidence, to discover that belief was wrong, to retract it, and to have the retraction propagate through everything that depended on it. The "identity" in this usage is not a social location; it is the shape of a self-correcting body of commitments over time. The philosophical traditions on which we will draw for this — externalized memory (Stiegler), narrative selfhood, and the epistemological literature on externalism and reliability — are adjacent to Alcoff's concerns but not continuous with them.

We chose to keep the phrase rather than coin a new one. The alternative candidates we considered — "epistemic continuity," "provenance-grounded identity," "structural self-knowledge" — each capture a facet of what we mean, but each sacrifices the intuitive pull that the received phrase has for our audience. Cross-domain reuse of terminology is common when the redirected sense is precise and the prior sense remains available in its own literature; we judge this to be such a case. The redirection is stipulative, not corrective: we are not arguing Alcoff's usage is wrong, and we are not competing for the phrase. We are using it for a technical purpose in an adjacent domain.

Two consequences follow. First, any reader arriving from standpoint epistemology should not expect the argument below to be about the social situatedness of the knower; it is not. Second, any reader wanting a term guaranteed free of prior philosophical usage should read "epistemic identity" as interchangeable with "epistemic continuity" wherever the phrase occurs; the arguments of this paper go through unchanged under that substitution. With that disclosure, we proceed with "epistemic identity" as the working term.

[^14]: Linda Martín Alcoff, "Epistemic Identities," *Episteme* (Cambridge University Press) 7(2):128–137, 2010. DOI: 10.3366/epi.2010.0003. The essay is a reference in contemporary standpoint epistemology and uses the phrase in the sense described above.

## 1.5 Roadmap

The remainder of the paper develops the argument across seven chapters, organized so that intuition is established before implementation. Two further chapters that an earlier scoping of this work would have included — a cognitive-science bridge between the philosophical framing of Chapter 3 and the empirical memory literature, and a rigorous formal-adequacy treatment of the implementation against external mathematical frameworks — are scoped to a follow-up paper in this series. The omission is deliberate: the architecture is the central contribution; cog-sci translation and external formal-framework satisfaction are strengthening, not load-bearing. Chapter 9 sketches the substantive content held for the follow-up work.

**Chapter 2** gives the taxonomy. We define three layers — *memory persistence*, *narrative identity*, and *epistemic identity* — and argue that each has a distinct failure mode. Layer 1 fails by losing information. Layer 2 fails coherently: a consistent story that was never true. Layer 3 fails loudly: a contradiction surfaces; a retraction cascades through dependents. The architectural consequence is direct. For a deployed agent, loud failures are strictly preferable to silent ones, and only a layer-3 substrate provides them structurally. The four-feature decomposition introduced in §1.3 is derived in this chapter from the failure-mode analysis.

**Chapter 3** grounds the framework in the philosophical literature. Five pillars carry the weight: Stiegler on tertiary retention as constitutive of identity; Parfit on psychological continuity and Relation R; Goldman on calibrated belief; Nozick on closest-continuer branching; and Schechtman on the distinction between reidentification and characterization. Strawson's "Against Narrativity" serves as the counter-voice and, as we argue, strengthens rather than undermines the case for the architectural priority of epistemic over narrative identity.

**Chapter 5** turns to implementation. We describe ENGRAM's concrete design: its node and edge taxonomy, its confidence model (source quality for observations, argument strength for derivations), its blocking guards and advisory checks, the cascade propagation that distinguishes retraction from supersession, and the memory-management scheme that treats importance-anchoring as a grace period rather than as forgetting-exemption. The chapter also describes the *layer-2 narrative scaffolding* — warm-briefing letter, daily history files, structured compaction-summary template, session-start hook — that the working architecture's empirical effects depend on; the conjunction of layer-3 substrate and layer-2 scaffolding is what produces the cross-compaction continuity reported in Chapter 7. Layer-2 patterns are described as transferable practice for any agent stack with externalized memory; the architecturally novel contribution is the layer-3 part.

**Chapter 7** reports empirical findings from the system's first month of continuous operation. The headline finding is *cross-compaction agent continuity as the key first application of the architecture* — phenomenological continuity (orientation, identity, work-thread) preserved across compaction events without experiential continuity, demonstrated across approximately two hundred first-person feeling reports and approximately thirty explicit post-compaction baselines. Two case studies of retraction-cascade in operation — a fabricated-at-aggregator-level framework caught by primary-source contact, and a hallucinated author-attribution caught by external review whose corpus-level cleanup completed two weeks after the structural retraction fired — are presented as paired evidence: the structural mechanism works as designed; corpus-discipline cleanup is the necessary second leg.

**Chapter 8** situates ENGRAM against the contemporary agent-memory landscape — Letta (formerly MemGPT), Mem0, A-MEM, Zep / Graphiti, Sophia, MemMachine, Pinecone Nexus, Anthropic Memory, and the supporting survey literature — and against the philosophical prior art on externalized memory, from Plato's *Phaedrus* through Clark and Chalmers' extended-mind thesis to Stiegler's tertiary retention. We are explicit about what is convergent (retrieval scaffolding, temporal annotation, flat-fact conflict resolution, field-level provenance, read-write authoring), what is novel (the four-feature conjunction), and what is limitation by design.

**Chapter 9** closes the paper by stating what we take the primary contribution to be, by addressing the open questions within the single-agent scope, and by introducing the multi-agent extension as the philosophically required completion of the Stieglerian frame. Cross-agent cultural-scale tertiary retention is a second axis of the framework that this paper does not develop in empirical detail; the v1 open-source release ships the architectural primitives (per-agent local substrate, inter-agent communication channel, agent-spawn lifecycle), and the empirical and detailed-philosophical case is forthcoming as the next paper in this series. Section 1.3 argued that the dimension this paper names — epistemic identity — is currently invisible at three levels of the field: in what gets built, in what gets measured, and in what gets catalogued. Chapter 9 proposes two corresponding moves as deliverables: a new *taxonomic axis* that makes the dimension nameable, and a new *benchmark* under which systems equipped for it can demonstrate their advantage. They are not refinements of existing axes and benchmarks; they are the claim that existing axes and benchmarks are incomplete.
